Generative Music

Two apps built not as market products, but as experiments with randomness and sound. No AI involved - every bit of generation runs on hand-built algorithms and parameters.

biaser

Ambient music that exists once and never repeats.

22 sound layers 5 moods nothing is ever saved
YouTube demo (4–9 min) - insert embed link

biaser generates ambient music. The idea draws on generative music: music that assembles itself, with no composer deciding in the moment what plays next.

The music is built from many short looping sound layers playing at once, constantly drifting slightly out of sync with each other - hence the name, from "bias," a shift.

You pick one of five moods, shown as color rather than words. From there the app decides everything on its own - you have almost no control, and that's the point. Nothing is saved: close the app, and the session is gone for good.

How it works

5 moods, each drawing from a pool of pre-built sound variations (16 in total). Each variation is 22 layers playing simultaneously, each with its own loop length (18–50 seconds, deliberately "ugly" numbers so the layers stay out of sync as long as possible) and a random starting point within its loop.

Layers belong to one of four instrument types: drone, pad, piano, and voice - each picks randomly from 3–5 prepared sound variants on launch.

The screen shows 22 progress bars, one per layer, each with a single control beneath it: a button that cycles the loop length through three states.

Frustrator

An app about not needing to control everything.

6 audio modules 6–11 parameters each randomized on every launch

Frustrator is a place to take a break from control and from chasing goals. Frustration here isn't burnout or helplessness - it's relaxation and acceptance. The app invites you to stop trying to control things and become an observer or a partner instead.

Your actions might change something, or they might not. Sometimes the effects arrive with a delay, and by then it's unclear what caused them. That's not a bug - that's the whole point. You can do nothing at all and still enjoy the music; Frustrator handles itself just fine, and that's no better or worse than any other way of engaging with it.

How it works

Six audio modules, each with six to eleven adjustable parameters. On launch, every parameter is set to a random value. By dragging smoke rings across the screen, you blindly redistribute those values - pushing some up, some down. Each time the app opens, the zones controlling different parameters get shuffled and reassigned at random. Just exploring it is its own kind of pleasant.
